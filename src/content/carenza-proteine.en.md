# Protein Deficiency: Clinical Impacts and Supplementation Strategies

## Protein Biochemistry

Proteins are macromolecules composed of amino acid chains linked by peptide bonds. Each protein has a primary structure (amino acid sequence), secondary structure (alpha-helix, beta-sheet), tertiary structure (3D folding), and quaternary structure (aggregation of subunits).

Amino acids are divided into:
- **Essential**: cannot be synthesized by the body (isoleucine, leucine, valine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan)
- **Non-essential**: can be synthesized endogenously
- **Conditionally essential**: needed under specific physiological conditions (arginine, glutamine, cysteine)

## Pathophysiology of Protein Deficiency

### Effects on Intestinal Epithelium

The intestinal epithelial lining completely renews every 3-5 days. This process requires specific amino acids:
- **Glutamine**: primary energy source for enterocytes
- **Arginine**: nitric oxide precursor for mucosal perfusion
- **Cysteine**: for glutathione synthesis (antioxidant)

Protein deficiency compromises:
1. **Tight junction integrity** → increased intestinal permeability (leaky gut)
2. **Reduced mucin secretion** → less protective barrier
3. **Decreased enzymatic activity** → malabsorption of other nutrients

### Systemic Effects

- **Immune system**: reduced immunoglobulin and lymphocyte production
- **Skeletal muscle**: progressive sarcopenia
- **Skin and appendages**: alopecia, dermatitis, non-healing wounds
- **Edema**: reduced plasma oncotic pressure

## Common Causes of Deficiency

1. **Inadequate dietary intake**: restrictive diets, unplanned vegetarianism/veganism
2. **Malabsorption**: celiac disease, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, SIBO
3. **Increased catabolism**: surgical stress, chronic infections, trauma
4. **Pathological losses**: protein-losing enteropathy, nephrotic syndrome

## Diagnosis

### Laboratory Markers
- **Serum albumin**: ~20-day half-life, long-term nutritional marker
- **Prealbumin (transthyretin)**: 2-3 day half-life, acute marker
- **Transferrin**: reflects iron reserves and protein status
- **Creatinine**: correlated with muscle mass

### Reference Values
- Albumin: 3.5-5.0 g/dL
- Prealbumin: 15-35 mg/dL
- Transferrin: 200-400 mg/dL

## Treatment and Supplementation

### Nutritional Goals

For healthy adults: **1.6-2.2 g/kg/day** (ISSN 2017)
For recovery from deficiency: **2.0-2.5 g/kg/day**

### Strategies for IBS

The low-FODMAP diet may limit some protein sources:
- **Legumes**: often high-FODMAP (galactans) → use firm tofu, rinsed canned lentils
- **Fresh dairy**: containing lactose → prefer lactose-free Greek yogurt, aged cheeses
- **Meat and fish**: low-FODMAP and high biological value

### Specific Supplementation

1. **Glutamine**: 5-10g/day for intestinal mucosal support
2. **Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)**: 5-10g pre/post exercise for athletes
3. **Hydrolyzed collagen**: 10g/day for connective tissue health

## Monitoring

Follow weekly:
- Body weight
- Muscle strength (handgrip test)
- Gastrointestinal symptoms
- Albumin/prealbumin levels (if available)
