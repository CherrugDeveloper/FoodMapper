## A beverage, not a cure

This traditional recipe combines bay leaf, sage, fennel and chamomile. It can be a pleasant warm drink and, for some people, warmth and hydration may provide temporary digestive comfort. Claims about the individual plants are based mainly on traditional use, laboratory studies or small clinical studies; evidence does not show that this tea cures IBS, reflux, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, infections or other diseases.

## Three preparation phases

### Phase 1: decoction

Place bay leaf, sage and fennel in **300 ml of water** (for example, one bay leaf, 2-3 sage leaves and a small amount of fennel seeds). Bring to the boil and **boil for 5 minutes with the lid on**.

### Phase 2: chamomile infusion

**Turn off the heat**, add chamomile, cover and leave to infuse for **7-8 minutes**. Chamomile is added after boiling to avoid unnecessarily prolonged heat exposure of its aromatic constituents.

### Phase 3: strain and drink

**Strain and drink lukewarm**, not scalding hot. Preferably prepare a fresh amount. The traditional dose specified here is **1 cup in the morning and 1 after the main meal**. Drink without sugar or with a minimal amount of honey. Honey remains a sugar and is not needed for a therapeutic effect.

## Plausible properties and evidence limits

- Warmth and water may contribute to hydration and a temporary feeling of relaxation.
- Fennel is traditionally used for mild gas and spasms; available clinical studies do not allow the effect to be generalized to everyone or to this recipe.
- Chamomile has been studied for relaxing and antispasmodic activity, but the quality and amount of evidence depend on the preparation and condition.
- Sage and bay leaf contain aromatic compounds and polyphenols studied in the laboratory; these findings should not be turned into promises of detoxification, weight loss or glucose control.

If symptoms persist, worsen or are new, the tea must not delay medical assessment. Severe pain, fever, persistent vomiting, blood in stool, jaundice, breathing difficulty or unintentional weight loss require care.

## Contraindications and interactions

- Avoid self-medication during pregnancy and breastfeeding, in children and with liver, kidney or neurological disease unless a professional advises it.
- Allergies are possible with chamomile and other Asteraceae (for example ragweed, arnica, chrysanthemum), fennel, sage or bay leaf. Stop if rash, swelling or breathing difficulty occurs; seek urgent help for a severe reaction.
- Concentrated sage preparations contain thujone; avoid highly concentrated preparations and oral essential oils. Do not ingest essential oils in this recipe.
- Chamomile may add to sedative effects and, according to clinical assessment, may interact with anticoagulants such as warfarin. Fennel and the other plants may have incompletely defined interactions.
- Anyone taking anticoagulants, sedatives, antiepileptics, diabetes or blood-pressure medicines, or multiple therapies should ask a doctor or pharmacist before regular use.
- The tea does not replace water, food, medicines or prescribed advice. People with diabetes should count honey and must not use the tea to correct hypoglycaemia.

## Medical disclaimer

This recipe is for information only and is not a cure, diagnosis or prescription. Thresholds, symptoms and regular use should be contextualized by a health professional. Safety can change with dose, plant form, allergies and medicines.
