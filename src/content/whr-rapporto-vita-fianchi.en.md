## What is WHR?

**WHR (Waist-to-Hip Ratio)** is the ratio between waist and hip circumference: **waist (cm) / hips (cm)**. It is a simple indicator of body-fat distribution and, in particular, of central adiposity relative to gluteofemoral adiposity. It does not directly measure visceral fat and is not a diagnosis.

## How to measure it

Measure the waist at the end of a normal breath out, without compressing the skin and with the tape horizontal. The site must be defined and reproducible: many protocols use the midpoint between the last palpable rib and the iliac crest, while others use the upper border of the iliac crest. Measure the hips at the maximum circumference of the buttocks. Take two readings, record conditions and light clothing, and repeat if they differ substantially.

The chosen protocol changes the result. For follow-up over time, use the same procedure every time.

## Cautious interpretation

Reference values are not universal. WHO proposed indicative cardiometabolic-risk cut-offs of **>0.90 in men and >0.85 in women**, but age, population, pregnancy, ethnicity, menopausal status and measurement method affect interpretation. Cut-offs do not replace blood pressure, glucose, lipids, medical history and an overall assessment.

A higher WHR is associated at population level with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. It is a statistical association, not an individual prediction. A value below a cut-off does not rule out risk: total fat, muscle mass, blood pressure, smoking and other factors still matter.

## WHR, BMI and other indicators

BMI describes weight relative to height, while WHR describes the relative distribution of circumferences. They can be informative together, but both have limitations: BMI cannot distinguish muscle from fat; WHR can change because of hip shape and does not quantify adipose volume. Waist circumference or WHtR may be easier to communicate in some settings.

## What to do with the result

An isolated result does not automatically call for a diet. If WHR is high or changes quickly, discuss physical activity, food pattern, sleep, blood pressure, glucose and lipids with a professional. The goal is not to chase a perfect cut-off, but to reduce risk through sustainable, person-appropriate actions.

## Limits and safety

Do not use WHR to self-diagnose obesity, metabolic syndrome or cardiovascular risk. It is not suitable for tracking changes during pregnancy, oedema, ascites or major local body changes. Cut-offs and interpretation should be contextualized by a health professional.
