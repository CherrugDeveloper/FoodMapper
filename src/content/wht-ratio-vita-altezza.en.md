## What is WHtR?

**WHtR (Waist-to-Height Ratio)** is the ratio between waist circumference and height, measured in the same units: **waist (cm) / height (cm)**. It summarizes the relationship between central adiposity and stature in a simple way. It is a screening and communication tool, not a diagnosis and not a direct measure of visceral fat.

## Measurement

Measure height without shoes while standing, and waist at the end of a normal breath out using a reproducible protocol. Do not pull the tape tight; some standards use the midpoint between the last rib and iliac crest, while others use the upper border of the iliac crest. Record the site, time and conditions and use the same procedure over time.

Calculate waist circumference divided by height. For example, 80 cm waist and 170 cm height give 0.47. The number has no unit.

## Indicative cut-offs and practical message

A commonly communicated rule is to **keep the waist below half of height** (WHtR <0.5). This is an indicative, not universal, cut-off: age, sex, ethnicity, growth, pregnancy, muscle distribution and measurement method affect the result. In children, thresholds must be interpreted using specific growth references rather than adult cut-offs.

Higher WHtR is associated in population studies with increased cardiometabolic risk. The ratio can be useful because it accounts for height, but it does not diagnose diabetes, hypertension or metabolic syndrome by itself. A value below 0.5 does not rule out risk, and a value above it does not establish disease.

## Comparison with BMI and WHR

BMI relates weight to height; WHR compares waist and hips; WHtR standardizes waist to stature. They provide different perspectives. BMI is affected by muscle mass, WHR by hip shape, and WHtR by measurement method and body proportions. The appropriate choice depends on the clinical context and monitoring goal.

## Limits and responsible use

Do not use one cut-off to judge a person's worth or to set treatment without guidance. Oedema, ascites, pregnancy, spinal deformity, major hydration changes or non-reproducible measurements can make the result difficult to interpret. If the ratio is high or changes along with blood pressure, glucose or weight, seek professional assessment.
