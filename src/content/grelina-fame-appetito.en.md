## What is ghrelin?

Ghrelin is a hormone produced mainly by the stomach and, to a lesser extent, by other tissues. Its acylated form activates the GHS-R1a receptor and contributes to growth-hormone secretion, food intake and brain reward circuits. It is often called the "hunger hormone", but that label is only a useful shorthand: hunger emerges from the integration of hormonal, neural, metabolic, environmental and psychological signals.

## How it contributes to appetite

Ghrelin may rise before a meal and tends to fall after eating. In the brain, particularly the hypothalamus, it facilitates NPY/AgRP neuron signals involved in food seeking. It also interacts with many other systems: stomach distension, the vagus nerve, cholecystokinin, GLP-1, PYY, leptin, insulin, food odors, availability, and meal-time habits.

Ghrelin does not simply say "eat": it can increase interest in highly palatable foods and link meals with reward. Actual behavior also depends on sleep, stress, social context, food access and learned preferences.

## Sleep, fasting and weight loss

Short sleep has been linked to changes in ghrelin and leptin and to more hunger in some controlled studies. Fasting and an energy deficit can also increase the drive to eat. After weight loss, several appetite signals may remain altered; this helps explain why weight maintenance can require ongoing strategies, but it does not mean regain is inevitable.

Responses vary. A single blood measurement does not establish how hungry a person feels and cannot identify which diet will work for them.

## Realistic strategies

There is no tea or supplement with a reliably demonstrated ghrelin effect for weight control. To support satiety and reduce impulsive decisions, it may help to:

- eat regular meals compatible with individual tolerance;
- include adequate protein and fiber, increasing fiber gradually when needed;
- choose food volume and energy density that fit the goal;
- protect regular sleep and physical activity;
- use a diary to distinguish physical hunger, cravings, stress and gut symptoms.

These suggestions do not require suppressing every hunger signal. Intense hunger, binge episodes, loss of control, unintentional weight loss or rigid restriction deserve discussion with a doctor, dietitian or psychologist.

## Interpretation limits

Ghrelin circulates in different forms (acylated and desacylated) and is sensitive to fasting, sampling time, food intake and laboratory method. Studies of drugs or agonists/antagonists in the ghrelin pathway are not recommendations for self-treatment. Ghrelin is not a diagnostic test for obesity, IBS or eating disorders.
